Randomized Benchmarking

● publishedlast tended Aug 12, 2026Quantum6 min

Q1What is it?

Randomized benchmarking estimates the average error rate of your gates by running many random sequences of Clifford gates, appending the one gate that should undo the whole sequence, and checking how often the qubit actually comes back. Plot survival probability against sequence length and you get an exponential decay; the decay rate is your average gate fidelity.

p(m) = A·fm + B   →   r = (1 − f)(d − 1)/d

Q2Why does it exist?

Process tomography tells you everything about a gate, but it's expensive and — worse — it can't separate the gate's errors from the errors in preparing and measuring the qubit (SPAM). RB's random twirling averages any noise channel into a depolarizing one, and the decay rate is immune to SPAM by construction. You trade complete information for one honest number.

Q3How does it connect?

Upstream, RB assumes the machinery of CPTP maps and the Clifford group. Sideways, its cousins fix its blind spots: Interleaved RB isolates a single gate, Cycle Benchmarking handles multi-qubit cycles, Mirror RB scales up. Downstream, RB numbers feed the error budgets that error correction thresholds are judged against — and the standard analysis quietly assumes Markovian noise, which is exactly where the research frontier begins.

Q4Where is it used?

Every hardware vendor quotes RB numbers on their spec sheets — it's the closest thing quantum hardware has to a standard benchmark, and the fine print (which variant, which gate set) is where roadmap claims get interesting. If you read one paper, read Magesan et al. 2011; if you read two, follow it with the non-Markovian RB work that shows what the standard protocol can't see.
